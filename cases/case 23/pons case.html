Case X - Weakness and abnormal eye movements

Case Where? What? Formulation Outcome

Case

A ---- woman was brought to hospital with weakness and slurred speech. 

She had been well that morning and driven to work. During a meeting she then slumped to one side and could not move her left arm or leg. She had drooping of the left corner of the mouth and was heavily slurring her words. Her colleagues called an ambulance and she was rushed to hospital. 

Her past medical history included asthma, hypertension and gastro-oesophageal reflux, but no neurological conditions.

On arrival 40 minutes after onset she was conscious and able to speak and obey commands. A brief history was taken during a rapid assessment in the emergency department. She reported no headache and had been well in the recent days, with no head trauma. 

On examination she had: 

  • Left hemiplegia (no voluntary movement) 

  • Left upper motor neuron (UMN) pattern facial weakness 

  • Heavily dysarthric speech but normal word content and comprehension 

  • Inability to abduct the right eye past the midline 

  • Blood pressure 230/120mmHg 

Within 2 minutes of arrival the patient suddenly became unconscious. Her pulses remained palpable, and she was still breathing.

On repeat examination she had: 

  • No response to vocal or tactile stimulation 

  • Symmetrical, reactive, small pupils without deviation in either eye

    • No spontaneous movements in her limbs  

    She was intubated for airway protection. 

    Where is the lesion? Where is the lesion?

    This is a neurological emergency. The patient's day started normally, then she developed sudden-onset left hemiplegia with dysarthria and right sixth nerve palsy, then rapidly developed coma. Her Glasgow coma scale is now 3 - she does not move, vocalise or open her eyes to any stimulus.

    We are looking for a lesion site that would explain all of this. We can start with the initial features.

    Left hemiplegia with UMN facial weakness and right sixth nerve palsy

    Left hemiplegia including the face - i.e. facio-brachio-crural paralysis - involves the motor tracts anywhere between their right hemispheric cortical origins and the right side of the pons, but no lower - the corticopontine UMN fibres then travel towards both facial nuclei in the dorsal pontine tegmentum - some cross, but remember that some remain on the same side to supply the upper facial muscles.

    Hence, this pattern of face-arm-leg weakness doesn't localise easily on its own, other than that it's above the medulla. Can the sixth nerve help?

    Sixth nerve (VI) palsy can arise anywhere between the abducens nucleus in the dorsal caudal pontine tegmentum and the nerve's final destination in the orbit, where it innervates the lateral rectus, so there are both intrapontine and peripheral sites that can be affected by lesions.

    Anybody who has made it this far into this site knows the basic principle that the combination of a cranial nerve palsy and contralateral limb signs localises to the brainstem - but let's consider this further.

    Abducens nucleus lesions tend to produce conjugate gaze paresis to the side of the lesion (i.e. the other eye cannot adduct), because the nucleus projects to nerve VI on the same side but also to the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) which lets it communicate with the contralateral nucleus of III in the midbrain, also needed for conjugate gaze. Hence a nuclear lesion causes conjugate gaze paralesis as above.

    This is different to our patient - her left eye can adduct during attempted right gaze. However, fascicular lesions within the pons - anywhere along the fibres before the nerve exits anteriorly in the pontomedullary junction - look the same as peripheral VI palsies. The clue to a lesion in the pons affecting the fascicles is cross-localisation with other structures - for example co-existing facial palsy, contralateral hemiparesis and/or hemianaesthesia.

    Nerve VI takes a very long and tortuous intracranial path. People often assume it has the longest path, but this is actually the trochlear (IV) nerve due to its decussation behind the midbrain - IV is the only nerve that decussates after its nucleus, and this winding path adds a lot of distance; other cranial nerves mostly travel directly for their target. The intracranial length of IV is 6cm, versus the abducens at 5cm - still long, and involving some uphill travel.

    It would help to briefly refresh on the path VI takes.

    From pons to lateral rectus: the path of nerve VI

    Brainstem - nucleus and fascicles

    After the nucleus, the fascicles of VI travel anteriorly through the pontine parenchyma. Their path is fairly central, not far off the midline, and they pass through the ventral pons (i.e. the base, or basis ponti). The pons is a 'busy neighbourhood' - so there are numerous structures the fascicles of VI pass near to.

    In the dorsal pons they include the fascicles of VII (which loop around the nucleus), the PPRF and MLF.

    Then as the fascicles travel forward they run near the medial lemniscus (ascending sensory pathway) in the central pons.

    In the ventral pons they pass the descending corticospinal fibres. At this level they are not one single tract but multiple individual fibres all separated, looking like small dots on axial cross-section. The fibres are wrapped in the transverse pontocerebellar fibres, massive structures that form the bulk of the pontine base. These pontocerebellar fibres illustrate just how much afferent input the cerebellum takes, much of it being linked to the descending signals from the brain to the pons and hence cerebellum. They externally wrap the corticospinal fibres but also run between them, separating them into bands. The fascicles of VI have to pass through these tranvserse fibres before they then exit the pons.

    It's worth remembering that the ventral pons also houses the descending corticobulbar fibres - the UMNs for facial muscles. As above, these then travel to the dorsal pons, and some decussate while others remain ipsilateral. It's possible to see an ipsilateral VI with contralateral UMN pattern facial weakness - often with contralateral limb weakness and sensory loss. This is known as Raymond syndrome.

    Intracranial pathway - prepontine cistern, skull base, Dorello's canal and cavernous sinus

    VI now exits the pontine base at the pontomedullary junction, now as a single nerve, entering the subarachnoid space filled with CSF - the prepontine cistern.

    It travels anteriorly and takes a sharp upward angle - it has to reach the cavernous sinus, along with III and IV, but III and IV leave the midbrain which is level with the sinus. In contrast, VI leaves the anterior pons low down at the junction with the medulla, then travels upwards along the clivus, a sloped bony structure in the skull base - it sits not far above the dural layer that lines the clivus. This relationship is very important - VI is vulnerable not only to skull base pathology (such as tumours) but also to anything that increases intracranial pressure ICP) - which can exert downward force on VI and effectively squash it against the skull base. VI palsy is common in high ICP states but is a false-localising sign - because the pathology driving the high ICP could be somewhere else altogether, for example a tumour in the contralateral hemisphere, causing high ICP and a unilateral VI palsy, falsely suggesting a lesion within or next to VI. Sometimes the palsy is bilateral, too. Even though other cranial nerves travel low down in the CSF space, they don't have this same relationship to high ICP - it's unique to VI and reflects its relationship to the clivus.

    VI then travels along the base of the skull over the sphenoid bone. It passes through a small space above the sphenoid called Dorello's canal, under a ligament (petrosphenoidal) which lies above it like a strap.

    Then it leaves the CSF space by piercing the dura at the posterior wall of the cavernous sinus. Here it travels with III, IV, V1, V2 and the sympathetic nerve supply to the pupil and eyelid. The other cranial nerves here are actually housed in the lateral wall whereas VI passes freely within the sinus, and the sympathetic is in the carotid plexus surrounding the internal carotid. The sympathetic leaves the carotid and joins VI. All of these pass through the superior orbital fissure to enter the orbit, except V2 which exits through the foramen rotundum.

    Orbit

    From here it's a short course to the lateral rectus muscle within the orbit.

    Where is the lesion?

    Putting this together, this patient either has:

    1. A lesion in a site that produces left hemiplegia (including UMN facial) and right VI palsy - and then coma

      2. OR

    2. A lesion somewhere else that explains the facial and limb weakness and also causes raised ICP, leading to a false-localising right VI palsy as a long-range effect

    If it's option A - a lesion that neatly explains both by cross-localisation (a focal 'precision strike') - then the only candidate is the right side of the pons, affecting the more ventral parts - hence the face and limb weakness - because no peripheral lesion of VI (i.e. outside of the brainstem) would explain the weakness. We will return to coma later.

    If it's B - it could be anywhere between the right hemispheric cortex and pons. It is possible - raised ICP could come on rapidly, with the VI palsy preceding loss of consciousness. The severe hypertension could also fit with this - it is often seen as a reaction to high ICP to maintain cerebral perfusion pressure, by the formula below:

    Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) = mean arterial pressure (MAP) - ICP

    It may not be possible to clinically say which is the case and clearly urgent imaging is needed - but since this site is about localisation, let's think briefly about pontine lesions that cause VI palsy.

    Key pontine anatomy ###################there's a lot of overlap here ##########################

    The pons is a massive transitional zone for many tracts and houses a number of important nuclei and other supranuclear structures. Its anatomy is complex, and many different collections of signs can arise from pontine lesions, so it's helpful to break it down. It's also a long structure in vertical terms, but we will focus on the interior (caudal) part.

    The inferior pons has the ipsilateral VI nucleus in the dorsal part of the tegmentum, close to the floor of the fourth ventricle. Its fascicles travel directly forward then leave the ventral (basal) pons at the pontomedullary junction. As above, nuclear lesions produce conjugate gaze paralysis (both eyes) to the side of the lesion, but fascicular lesions only affect abduction of the ipsilateral eye.

    The facial nerve (VII) nucleus is anterior and lateral to the VI nucleus. Its fascicles form a 'loop' - they travel backwards from the nucleus on 45 degree angle then wrap around the VI nucleus medially - then travel behind it, forming the facial colliculus (a mound in the floor of the fourth ventricle). Then they travel anterolaterally and exit the lateral pons.

    The corticospinal tracts are further forward in the ventral pons, but unlike in the midbrain or the medulla, they are not in the most ventral part - there are large bands of horizontally travelling pontocerebellar fibres that surround the corticospinal tracts and form the bulk of the pons when seen from the front. They give a sense of just how important the cerebellum is given the amount of input the pons sends it.

    The corticobulbar tracts (UMN facial) are next to the corticospinal ones. They then pass backwards and synapse in the facial nuclei. Each tract contributes both ipsilaterally and contralaterally, so some fibres decussate. All fibres supplying the lower part of the face decussate, whereas only half of those to the upper part do - some remain on the same side. In other words, the upper half of the face has bilateral UMN input, which is why it is spared in UMN lesions.

    The medial lemniscus fibres are right in the middle of the pons. The spinothalamic fibres are further dorsally and laterally.

    In the very medial parts of the dorsal pontine tegmentum are two structures relevant to horizontal gaze: the medial longitudinal fasciculus and the paramedian pontine reticular formation. We have explored these in earlier cases in relation to internuclear ophthalmoplegia - see case xxxxxxxxxxxxxx to recap.

    Pontine lesions

    Clearly, depending on the lesion site, different features arise. This allows us to localise pontine lesions. This subject is complex as many combinations of features exist and several have eponymous names to add to the list of things we have to remember. Worse still, they are inconsistent between sources. In my opinion they are not worth using.

    We can simplify it with some shorthand 'rules' however.

    Firstly, paralysis of conjugate gaze (both eyes) is seen with dorsal lesions, as is internuclear ophthalmplegia, but not ventral lesions - the key structures are further back in the dorsal tegmentum.

    Lower motor neuron (LMN) facial weakness is seen with more dorsal and lateral lesions, because the fascicles don't travel ventrally.

    Limb weakness suggests are more central or ventral lesion. So does UMN facial weakness (descending corticobulbar fibres before their synapse with the facial nuclei on both sides).

    This patient has left UMN facial weakness - so the problem must affect the fibres ventrally, or centrally as they pass backward towards the nuclei. A right-sided lesion would make sense, affecting the fibres proximal to their nuclear destination. She also has left hemiplegia which would fit with the right-sided corticospinal tracts being involved - they will not decussate until the pyramids in the caudal medulla.

    The best explanation - if this is all due to a focal lesion rather than the VI being false-localising - is a right ventral, or central, pontine lesion affecting the VI fascicles and the UMN corticobulbar and corticospinal tracts. For what it's worth, this is known as Raymond syndrome.

    One other clue supports this being a pons lesion - the dysarthria is severe. Dysarthria is a common feature in strokes which affect the UMN corticobulbar fibres, but speech is usually still intelligible. Pontine lesions however produce severe dysarthria, sometimes reducing speech to an almost unintelligble 'growl'. This is probably due to a combination of motor and cerebellar fibres being damaged.

    What about coma?

    The fact the patient rapidly lost consciousness doesn't tell us this is due to a pons lesion or another lesion elsewhere causing fulminantly raised ICP - but it's certainly compatible with the pons.

    There are projections from the pons that are involved in consciousness - the reticular activating system (RAS). They project from the reticular formation in the upper brainstem (pons and midbrain) towards the thalamus. Lesions can cause unconsciousness.

    The fibres are in the upper (rostral) pons, whereas the VI palsy suggests caudal pons. It could therefore be that this lesion started in the inferior pons and then expanded upwards, damaging the RAS fibres.

    Summary

    If this is all due to one lesion, it's best explained by a right central/ventral caudal pontine lesion that expanded and caused coma. Otherwise, there may be a lesion somewhere in the right brain or upper brainstem that caused raised ICP, leading to a VI palsy as a false-localising sign and then coma.

    ====== WHAT 

    This lesion was sudden-onset and evolved rapidly, with coma developing in less than an hour from onset. It is almost certainly vascular - little else would come on this quickly. Mass lesions can sometimes cause sudden deterioration due to hydrocephalus, particularly if the fourth ventricle is obstructed, but it seems unlikely the patient would be entirely asymptomatic before this, and hemiplegia would be unusual.

    Both ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke could present this way. The ventral pons can develop infarction from occlusion of the paramedian vessels, and the same vessels can rupture leading to haemorrhage.

    It's less common to see rapid deterioration to coma in pontine ischaemic strokes. However, basilar artery thrombosis can initially affect the pons then lead to coma as the thrombus migrates upwards to the basilar tip. Miller-Fisher (the father of all things brainstem stroke localisation-related) described this as 'herald hemiparesis' in 1988 - unilateral weakness preceding bilateral signs and coma as the thrombus ascends to knock out structures involved in consciousness.

    On the other hand a pontine haemorrhage could easily produce coma due to a rapidly-expanding haematoma - affecting the reticular activating systems and extending upwards into the midbrain. Pontine haemorrhage is very dangerous - if there is coma the chances of survival are minimal, sadly.

    It is also possible that the patient has an ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke elsewhere leading to high ICP and a false-localising VI then coma. Of the two, haemorrhagic stroke is more likely, but it would have to be large. If within the hemisphere, the coma usually follows massive herniation - and the classic marker of this is not VI palsy but III palsy, as the temporal lobe uncus herniates over the tentorial edge (see case XXX). It seems less likely somehow that the VI is false-localising here given the acuity of everything developing all at once. A focal pontine lesion fits too nicely - it would be surprising if the VI were false-localising.

    Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH)

    This can lead to rapid-onset coma and sometimes has focal signs accompanying it. VI palsy could be seen due to high ICP. However the situation here isn't a typical presentation of SAH - the patient had no headache, and sudden-onset hemiplegia is unusual.

    Extradural or subdural haematoma

    These usually follow head trauma although some subdural haematomas are spontaneous, for example in people with coagulopathy. They can cause rapid-onset focal deficits accompanied by headache and altered mental status and, if untreated, progression to coma. Here there was no recent trauma however so they seem unlikely.

    Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST)

    CVST is often associated with VI palsy due to raised ICP - sometimes bilaterally. The presentation is usually severe headache, which this patient didn't have. Papilloedema is evident, a non-specific marker of raised ICP - although this patient didn't undergo fundoscopy. It's not common to see sudden-onset hemiplegia in CVST however, nor coma - deep site thrombosis in the internal cerebral veins can affect the thalamus causing coma but hemiplegia would be unusual. When it develops it's often a complication of haemorrhagic transformation of venous infarctions in the cortex, which takes some time to emerge.

    Non-vascular causes

    Occasionally, previously-silent tumours present in a stroke-like manner, with no preceding symptoms then sudden-onset of deficits. This is often due to the massive development of cerebral oedema, which may reach a 'critical mass' and compromise structures, typically in the cortex. It's a little unusual for this to happen mid-day however; often people are found in the morning having been well the night before.

    Tumours can also cause seizures, with sudden-onset of post-ictal deficits and drowsiness. They can also bleed, causing an intracranial haemorrhage, again manifesting like a stroke. Finally they may obstruct CSF channels, especially the fourth ventricle - causing rapid deterioration with hydrocephalus (typically with headache, vomiting and reduced consciousness).

    It's less likely that this is a tumour - to go from no symptoms and totally well to sudden hemiplegia and VI palsy, then rapid deterioration (minutes) to coma, is an unusual trajectory.

    Obviously, tumours are an important cause of ICP and hence false-localising VI palsy.

    Other causes

    The brainstem is a vulnerable site for inflammatory brainstem lesions, but this doesn't seem likely. It's a common target in multiple sclerosis, but never this fulminantly, likewise other neuroinflammatory diseases such as neuromyelitis optica. Brainstem encephalitis has various causes including infections (such as Listeria and Enterovirus) or autoimmune causes including variants of Guillain-Barré syndrome and paraneoplastic diseases. While these might cause focal deficits and altered consciousness they wouldn't present this rapidly. Behcet's disease can cause midbrain lesions but the pons is atypical. CLIPPERS (chronic lymphocytic inflammation with pontine perivascular enhancement responsive to steroids) affects the pons - but the tempo is far slower.

    Toxic and metabolic brainstem insults exist - the most feared is osmotic demyelination which tends to affect the central pons, due to (------something about osmoreceptors and BBB--------------). This comes on amid osmotic fluid shifts, especially with rapid correction of chronic hyponatraemia, and sometimes after liver transplantation. It can cause paralysis, including bilateral, with ophthalmoplegia and anarthria - the locked-in state. Here there is nothing to suggest any vulnerability to this, and the onset is so rapid it doesn't seem likely. (ANY OTHER BRANISTEM TOXIC SYNDROMES ???)

    A range of neurodegenerative diseases affect the brainstem, for example Parkinson's and progressive supranuclear palsy, causing problems such as parkinsonism, eye movement disorders and autonomic dysfunction - but they never present this fast, so there is no way this is neurodegenerative.

    Summary - what is the lesion?

    This feels very likely to be a vascular lesion in the right, central pons - one capable of rapid deterioration to coma.

    OUTCOME

    The patient was taken for a CT scan immediately after intubation. This showed a large intrapontine haemorrhage, centred towards the right, with extension rostrally into the midbrain. The pons was distended due to the internal blood. There was no evidence of hydrocephalus. 

    The patient was discussed with the on-call neurosurgery team. Unfortunately there was no surgical option possible to treat this - the blood was intraparenchymal rather than in the ventricle or in the space surrounding the cerebellum, both of which can sometimes be addressed surgically. 

    She was taken to the intensive care unit and ventilated for 24 hours in addition to receiving aggressive blood-pressure lowering with intravenous beta blockers and nitrates. 

    The next day she was reviewed after several hours with sedation paused. The examination found the following signs:

    • She remained ventilator-dependent and deeply unresponsive

    • She made little response to noxious stimuli applied to limbs, sternum or supraorbital notches.

    • On oculocephalic testing her eyes moved with the head, indicating absent bilateral vestibulo-ocular reflexes

    • She had no response to blink reflex testing in either eye

    • Pupils were non-reactive to light and mid-sized

      • While the patient did not satisfy formal criteria for death by neurological criteria (sometimes referred to as 'brainstem death'), it was felt that this haemorrhage had caused overwhelming neurological damage and was unsurvivable. The team discussed her condition with her relatives who were clear that the patient would not wish to be kept alive in the event of such an illness. The team made the decision to withdraw life-support and the patient passed away that afternoon with her family present.

      Final diagnosis

      Sudden-onset left hemiplegia and UMN facial weakness with right abducens palsy due to a right pontine haemorrhage, followed by coma then development of severe brainstem damage, with a fatal outcome.

      Key points
      1. Nerve VI is long and can be affected by lesions anywhere between its nucleus and fascicles in the pons through to its destination in the orbit. It can also be affected by elevated ICP as a false-localising sign

      2. VI lesions can be cross-localised with other features, if present, to find the site. In this case, ipsilateral VI with contralateral hemiplegia localises to the inferior pons. 

      3. 5-10% of intracerebral haemorrhages affect the pons. These may be mild, but if they are large and produce early coma, as in this case, then survival is unlikely.
